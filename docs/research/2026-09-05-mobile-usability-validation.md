# Mobile usability validation

## Purpose and method

Audience clarification: the product owner defines adolescents with routine smartphone/app experience as ManaWG's primary audience. Test familiar app behavior, efficient first/repeat use and balanced density within that audience. Clear task meaning and accessibility basics remain part of quality; the study is not centered on teaching basic phone operation.

Validate the phone-only redesign with two **small, moderated, task-based rounds**: prototype, then working app. This is formative research to find and fix problems, not a statistically representative benchmark. NN/g defines usability testing as observing realistic users attempt realistic activities; task wording should provide context without revealing the UI path ([Usability Testing 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/); [Task Scenarios](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/task-scenarios-usability-testing/)). Pilot the script with one or two representative people before the round ([Writing Tasks](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/test-tasks-quant-qualitative/)).

Test labels, grouping and density by asking participants to locate and complete goals without coaching. Do not apply a “three-click” rule or a fixed number-of-items rule: record whether the wording, grouping, scanability and retained context let people choose confidently. W3C requires headings and labels to describe topic or purpose, and says clear labels help orientation ([Understanding SC 2.4.6](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/headings-and-labels.html)). Consistent navigation placement and labels support learning and repeat use ([WAI predictability guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/fundamentals/accessibility-principles/)).

Use a facilitator who opens with “we are testing the design, not you,” asks neutral follow-ups, and does not rescue until a task is abandoned. Invite think-aloud, but do not treat silence as success. Observe actual young users doing WG tasks; automatic interaction tests and an owner's preference do not establish how the target audience experiences the app ([Usability Testing 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/)).

## Proposed rounds and participants

Target eight sessions for each evaluated design version with young current or prospective WG users who routinely use smartphone apps. Include people new to shared-household organization and those with WG experience, relevant payer/chore/communication roles, and iPhone/Android use. Offer accessibility accommodations as needed. Smaller exploratory rounds can find problems, but do not satisfy the eight-person project checkpoint. Report raw outcomes and the participant context; do not assume app familiarity proves understanding of the financial consequences. This sample definition follows the owner's audience, not a claim that every young person has identical skills.

For return use, invite the same people back after 2–7 days for two equivalent tasks with new data and no refresher; this interval is a practical project choice. A replacement group tests first use, not retention. Use participants' own phones where possible. Recruitment, recording and sessions have not occurred; no outreach is authorized by this report.

## Task prompts

Use neutral scenarios in participants' comfortable language (German for this app); replace names/amounts with test data. Do not require one path when another achieves the goal correctly.

1. **Shopping:** “The WG needs coffee and toilet paper. Add these to the shared shopping list so someone can buy them.”
2. **Expense split:** “You paid EUR 36 for shared cleaning supplies. Record it and split it equally among the people who live here.”
3. **Chore:** “You have cleaned the kitchen. Record that and find out who is responsible next.”
4. **Notices versus chat:** “Leave an instruction about where the spare rubbish bags are kept, so a new flatmate can find it next week.” Then separately: “Ask whether anyone is shopping today.” Observe discoverability and understanding of the two uses; do not invent an official-notice role.
5. **Invite:** “A new flatmate, Sam, is moving in. Invite Sam to this WG.”
6. **Failure and return:** “While saving an expense, your connection drops. Show what you expect to happen, then return later and check whether it was saved before trying again.”
7. **Return visit:** after the delay, “Without a tour, check what needs your attention today and complete one appropriate action.”

## Comparison, measures, and project targets

If comparing two navigation structures, prefer **between-subjects** allocation: each participant sees one structure, randomly assigned, because it reduces learning effects. A within-subject comparison uses fewer people but requires counterbalanced order; it still cannot erase carryover, familiarity, or a participant recognising the same task. Keep task goals and data equivalent, use different names/amounts where a goal repeats, and avoid comparing raw completion times across device/network conditions. NN/g describes this trade-off and calls for randomisation/counterbalancing when multiple conditions are shown ([Between- vs. Within-Subjects](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/between-within-subjects/); [Validity guidance](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/internal-vs-external-validity/)).

Record: completion outcome (independent / completed with prompt / failed), first place looked, wrong turns, time to first confident action and completion, backtracking, help use, errors/recovery, observed confusion/overload, exact label or card that caused it, think-aloud quotes, post-task ease (1–5), and whether the participant can explain where they would return next time. Screen/audio recording requires informed consent; let participants pause or decline recording.

**Project targets, not research facts:** on the selected version, each core task is completed independently by at least 7/8 participants from the defined audience. Record unnecessary steps, explanations and interruptions as usability findings alongside missing information. No unresolved misunderstanding of who owes/pays, whether money moves, or which account/WG is affected is acceptable, even once. Repeated core-task blockage requires correction. Apply the same completion target to both return tasks; report attrition as missing evidence. These counts guide iteration, not population-level certainty. A miss requires a design correction and targeted retest, not coaching counted as success.

## Limits

Prototype sessions are useful early, but simulated money, invitations, offline failures and household consequences reduce realism. Moderated sessions can also increase effort because someone is watching; unmoderated testing is especially weak for early prototypes and imaginative or emotional tasks ([NN/g on unmoderated tests](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/unmoderated-usability-testing/)). Therefore, follow the prototype round with the working app and later review real, consented support/usage signals; neither small round alone proves long-term adoption or population-level performance.
